A fast radio burst is a flash of radio emission that lasts a few milliseconds. The
first one was reported in 2007. The bursts are bright enough to cross the universe,
and they arrive as a short, sharp pulse with no warning.

FRB 20240304B was detected by the MeerKAT telescope on 4 March 2024. On 8 October
2026, NASA published the result of a Webb observation that measured how far away it
came from. It is the farthest known fast radio burst.

## How far away it is

The host galaxy has a redshift of 2.148, according to a spectrum taken with Webb's
NIRSpec instrument. In plain terms, the light from the galaxy has been stretched by
the expansion of the universe, and the stretch puts the galaxy about 3 billion years
after the Big Bang.

That distance is the headline, but the galaxy is the real finding.

## A galaxy that is too small

The team expected the host to be a big, well-formed galaxy full of stars. Most fast
radio bursts with a known host sit in massive, star-forming galaxies. This one does
not.

The host is a small dwarf galaxy, still forming stars, and it is about 1,000 times
less massive than the team expected. Ben Stappers of the University of Manchester,
one of the co-authors, put it plainly: the host "sticks out in the whole galaxy sample
that we have."

Lead author Manisha Caleb of the University of Sydney put the expectation this way:
"We thought it would be a big, nicely formed galaxy with lots of stars." The data
showed a small one.

## Two explanations, one of them ruled out

Two explanations have been on the table for a while. One is the merger of two neutron
stars. The other is a single young, highly magnetic neutron star, a magnetar, that
produces the burst through starquakes shortly after a supernova.

The team leans against the merger. Caleb's words on that were direct: "Our work
suggests that it's very unlikely that this FRB was produced by a merger." Neutron star
mergers take billions of years to happen after the stars form. That timing favours
older galaxies, and this host is young.

A young magnetar fits a young galaxy. That is the argument, and it is an argument
rather than a proof. The team is saying the evidence points one way, not that the
question is closed.

## Why this matters beyond one burst

Working out where fast radio bursts come from is hard because the bursts are short
and they leave almost nothing behind. Matching one to a host galaxy is the first step,
and the host then tells you what kind of object could have made the burst.

A host at this distance also has a second use. The light from a burst passes through
the material between us and the source on the way, and that material leaves a signature
in the signal. Bursts from distant hosts are useful for measuring the gas that fills
the space between galaxies.

NASA's own release describes the origin as a suggestion, not a settled result, and
that is the honest way to read it. One burst, one galaxy and one spectrum make a strong
case. They do not make a catalogue.
