Planets are built from collisions, and the Solar System's own story is full of them.
The Moon is thought to have formed after a Mars sized body struck the young Earth,
but nobody has a direct record of that impact. What astronomers can look at is the
debris left behind when collisions happen around other stars. On 1 October 2026 a
team using Webb reported on 21 of the rarest of those systems.

They are called extreme debris discs. They sit around young stars, and they are
unusual in the mid infrared, where Webb works best, because they are unusually warm
and their brightness changes over time. The team, led by Kate Su of the Space Science
Institute in Boulder, Colorado, said the sample is the largest of its kind.

## Two kinds of dust

The most useful thing about the dust is what it is made of. The discs fall into two
chemical families, and the split is about silica.

About one third of the discs are silica rich. The team says these likely formed from
high energy impacts between Mars sized bodies. The remaining two thirds are silica
poor, which suggests a gentler kind of collision, such as a grazing hit between
bodies about the size of the Moon. The silica rich discs appear only around stars
younger than about 300 million years, which is the detail that makes the split
interesting.

Su put the value of the sample simply. "This is the first time we have gathered
enough systems to truly understand this subclass that we call extreme debris discs,"
she said. "Before Webb, we had limited information."

## Why the young stars matter

If the silica rich discs come only from the youngest stars, then the violent impacts
happen early and then stop. The team's co author Attila Moór of Konkoly Observatory
in Budapest put the expectation directly: "We expect no silica rich systems among
older extreme debris discs." The sample has only three discs that fit the age
criteria, which is why he added that it would be "nice to observe more of these
systems to confirm our hypothesis."

That caution is the honest part. A hypothesis that rests on three objects is a
hypothesis, not a settled timeline. The study is useful because it names the test
and the number of cases needed to pass or fail it.

## Watching planets that are too small to see

The other problem is that the things doing the colliding are too small to observe
directly. Ágnes Kóspál of Konkoly Observatory, who co authored the study, put it
plainly: "We have no other way to study these planetary embryos directly because they
are too small." The dust is the evidence, and the spectra are the fingerprint.

Kóspál said that the most exciting part for her was seeing the mid infrared emission
and the spectral features with Webb, because they "allowed us to identify their
compositions." That identification is what turns a faint smudge around a distant star
into a record of a collision.

## What it does and does not say about the Solar System

The release connects the findings to the Solar System's formation. Su said that how
rocky planets formed and how giant planets evolved "are part of the broader story of
the Solar System's formation." The disks are a way of checking that story against
other systems, and they do not replay it.

Some outlets have drawn a straight line from these discs to the Moon's origin. The
NASA and ESA releases do not make that claim, and the link should be read as a
possibility that the study allows, not a conclusion it reached.

The study was published in *The Astrophysical Journal* the same week the release came out. For a piece
of work built on 21 objects, the honest reading is that it has found a pattern worth
testing, and it has said exactly how the pattern would be shown to be wrong.
